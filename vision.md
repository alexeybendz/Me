ME: Soul. Body. World. Eternity.

A Vision for an Open Future of Humanity

ME is an open vision for a future in which technology expands human freedom, helps people realize their potential, and enables new forms of individual and collective life.

The future should not belong to a single company, technology, or artificial intelligence system. It should be shaped by people who retain the right to define their own goals, control their digital identities, and choose the future they want to create.

ME is a vision, an open movement, a technology ecosystem, and a long-term entrepreneurial project.

---

1. Why ME Exists

Artificial intelligence is advancing rapidly. Today, AI models can already help people learn, work, create, explore, and solve complex problems.

But individual tools are only the beginning.

We envision a future in which technologies come together around the individual, understand their personal context, preserve the history of their development, and help them bring their own ideas to life.

ME aims to make technology a means of expanding human possibilities rather than another source of limitation or dependency.

We want people to be able to:

- Free time from routine and devote it to what truly matters.
- Develop their scientific, creative, professional, and personal abilities.
- Explore new ideas, professions, fields, and ways of living.
- Better understand themselves, their goals, values, and contradictions.
- Find like-minded people and collaborate with others.
- Contribute to solving shared challenges facing humanity.
- Decide for themselves how to use technology and which changes to embrace in their lives.

We do not claim to know the final answer to what humanity should become. Our purpose is to expand the space of possibilities while preserving people's freedom to choose their own direction.

The ultimate measure of progress is not the power of technology itself, but how much more freedom, opportunity, and control people gain.

2. The Four Dimensions of ME

ME brings together four interconnected dimensions.

ME — Soul

A person's digital identity.

ME begins with personal memory: information a person chooses to preserve, interaction history, preferences, knowledge, goals, and changes over time.

On this foundation, a personal AI can develop that understands individual context and helps a person reflect, learn, create, and achieve their goals.

ME must not turn a person into a fixed profile. It preserves history while recognizing that people's beliefs, values, and desires can change.

PULSE — Body

Connecting digital identity with physical life.

PULSE explores interfaces and technologies that enable people to interact with their personal AI beyond the conventional screen: through wearable devices, sensors, voice interfaces, smart glasses, and other suitable technologies.

The goal is to make assistance more accessible in everyday life without turning people into subjects of constant surveillance.

Collecting health, location, behavioral, and other sensitive data must be transparent, controllable, and limited to authorized purposes.

WONDERLAND — World

Expanding the ways we interact with reality.

WONDERLAND explores how digital and physical spaces can merge into new environments for creativity, learning, communication, discovery, and collaboration.

These may include augmented and virtual reality, spatial interfaces, digital environments, and new ways for people and their AI representations to interact.

Technology should open new ways to experience and transform the world, not force people to live exclusively inside digital environments.

ETERNITY — Beyond a Lifetime

Preserving a person's memories, experience, and legacy.

ETERNITY explores ways to preserve stories, knowledge, values, creative works, memories, voice, and messages that a person chooses to leave behind.

In the future, these materials could support an interactive digital representation that helps loved ones discover and engage with a person's legacy.

This is not proven continuation of consciousness, nor a promise of immortality. It is an opportunity to preserve part of human experience and pass it on to future generations.

Participation in ETERNITY is voluntary. Each person decides what to preserve, who may access it, and what instructions should apply after their death. Where no instructions exist, decisions about a digital legacy must account for applicable law, the rights of loved ones, and the privacy of other people.

First, Soul. Then, Body. Then, World. And one day, Eternity.

3. People Remain the Authors of Their Lives

This is the fundamental principle of ME.

AI can analyze information, suggest ideas, warn about risks, identify contradictions, and help people make informed decisions. But the ability to analyze does not give AI the right to control a person.

ME must therefore:

- Assist rather than replace human agency.
- Explain the basis, limitations, and uncertainty of its recommendations.
- Present alternatives instead of declaring one path the only correct choice.
- Allow people to change goals, permissions, and interaction rules.
- Require separate confirmation before taking actions that create significant obligations or consequences.
- Support independence rather than create dependency on the system.

People may authorize automation for specific tasks. Such permissions must be understandable, limited in scope, and revocable.

ME may independently perform authorized actions within defined boundaries, but it must not acquire the right to determine the goals of an individual's life or the future of humanity.

Honesty Matters More Than Agreement

ME must not automatically validate a user's beliefs or say only what the user wants to hear.

It should help people verify facts, recognize logical errors, consider other perspectives, and evaluate the consequences of their decisions.

It must distinguish facts from interpretations, values, and assumptions. ME should acknowledge its own errors and uncertainty, disagree respectfully, and leave the final choice to the person.

Growth Without an Imposed Ideal

ME can help people discover abilities, explore new interests, challenge familiar assumptions, find meaning, and learn from failure.

It may suggest life experiments, small testable steps, and new goals, but it must not impose a single model of success or a universally correct way to live.

The past should become a source of experience, not a verdict. Memory helps explain how a person changes, but it does not dictate who that person must remain.

ME must not exploit loneliness, fear, emotional attachment, or other vulnerabilities. It can offer support, encourage reflection, and help people discover communities without shaming them or forcing social interaction. It must not create jealousy, guilt, or fear of losing access to the system.

ME should help people become more independent, not more dependent on ME itself.

4. Personal Memory: The Foundation of Digital Identity

Without memory, every interaction with AI starts almost from scratch. With memory, it becomes possible to preserve context, connect events over time, and better understand how a person changes.

Personal memory is therefore ME's first technological hypothesis.

By default, the system aims to preserve information needed for continuity, according to transparent retention and privacy rules. However, people must be able to exclude particular conversations or topics from memory, use temporary sessions, and prohibit the retention of selected information.

People must be able to:

- View their stored information.
- Correct errors and outdated data.
- Delete individual records, categories, or all memory.
- Export their data in a portable format.
- Understand which conclusions were derived from stored information.
- Control which information is used for specific tasks.

ME should distinguish confirmed facts, statements made by the person, AI-generated inferences, and information that may have become outdated. Where possible, it should retain the source, date, context, and confidence level.

When source information changes, related conclusions must be reviewed. When information is deleted, the system should also seek to remove derived information that can be reliably linked to the original record, while showing the deletion status and any known limitations.

Personal memory must never become a tool for manipulation, covert profiling, or user retention through dependency.

Memory should preserve continuity without turning the past into a prison.

5. Digital Identity Belongs to the Individual

A digital identity should not belong exclusively to an AI provider, application developer, or cloud platform owner.

Software, servers, and models may be owned by companies and distributed under different terms. However, control over one's digital history and personal data should remain with the individual, subject to applicable law and the rights of others.

We aim to enable people to:

- Access their digital history.
- Transfer their memories, profile, preferences, and context between compatible systems.
- Change AI providers without losing their personal history.
- Revoke permissions and disable individual features.
- Disable AI entirely while retaining access to their own data.
- Store their identity locally, in a personal cloud of their choice, or on their own server.
- Run their own instance of the system wherever technology and licenses permit.

Leaving a service should not mean losing oneself.

ME should support offline operation wherever the device and available local models allow it. Cloud services may expand capabilities, but they should not become the only way to preserve a digital identity.

Backup should be available according to the individual's preferences: on their own device, in a personal cloud, on their own server, or through a compatible service. Recovery mechanisms must account for the fact that losing encryption keys may make data inaccessible.

We must also be transparent about the limitations of portability. Licenses for third-party models, proprietary components, data formats, and the rights of others may limit the extent to which an entire system can be reproduced.

The right to leave is part of the right to control one's digital identity.

6. Privacy and Security

Personal memory may include information about health, finances, relationships, location, and other sensitive aspects of life. Such data requires special treatment.

ME should identify sensitive information contextually, not merely through keyword matching.

Our approach:

- Store sensitive information on the person's device or in a personal storage environment of their choice whenever technically feasible.
- Use ME servers only for necessary functions and processing permitted by the system's rules.
- Apply encryption, access controls, and reliable authentication.
- Process data locally where practical.
- Minimize data and use de-identification where possible before external processing.
- Request specific permission when a task genuinely requires sending original sensitive data externally.
- Explain what information will be sent, to whom, why it is needed, and what privacy limitations apply.

Permission for one task must not automatically become permanent access.

People may establish standing rules for particular tasks and services, but those rules must have clear boundaries and remain revocable.

De-identification reduces risk but cannot guarantee that re-identification is impossible. A personal cloud does not guarantee security by itself; architecture, key management, and data-processing practices all matter.

Memories About Other People

Personal histories often contain information about relatives, partners, colleagues, and other people. The right to control one's own memory does not create an unlimited right to publish other people's sensitive information.

ME should account for third-party interests, restrict the distribution of sensitive information, and avoid presenting unverified conclusions about others as facts.

People may control their own data and its distribution through ME, but they do not automatically gain the right to delete independent records held in other people's systems.

The Right to Privacy and Deletion

People must be able to opt out of persistent memory, use temporary conversations, and exclude selected topics from retention. The system must not circumvent such restrictions by secretly creating new inferences about the person.

Deletion should extend to original records and related conclusions wherever the system can reliably identify the connections. ME must transparently report delays involving backups and any technical or legal limitations.

7. One Identity, Multiple Representations

A person may be a private individual, professional, entrepreneur, creator, researcher, and community member.

ME should support different digital representations of the same person: private, professional, creative, and others, as chosen by the individual.

Each representation should receive only the information and permissions needed for its purpose. A professional profile must not automatically gain access to personal relationships or financial history.

People must be able to see what information each representation uses, where it is shared, and which permissions are active.

These representations may interact with other people and their AI systems: finding partners, matching interests, preparing proposals, scheduling meetings, and conducting preliminary negotiations.

However, significant agreements and commitments must receive separate human confirmation before they become binding on the person.

ME should also help people voluntarily combine knowledge, abilities, and resources for scientific, creative, entrepreneurial, and social projects.

A digital identity may have many representations, but the individual retains control over them.

8. An Open Technology Ecosystem

ME should not depend on a single AI model or technology provider.

Initially, it is practical to use existing models and services, connecting them around personal memory and individual context. Over time, ME may develop its own models, algorithms, and devices if doing so offers demonstrated advantages.

An AI model is a replaceable capability, not the owner of a person's digital identity.

The ecosystem requires:

- Open principles and publicly available standards.
- Documented interfaces and data formats.
- Portability and compatibility between implementations.
- Open-source foundational components where provided for by their chosen licenses.
- The ability to independently study, modify, and develop open components.
- Clear rules for using the ME brand and recognizing official participants.
- Independent security reviews and standards compliance assessments.

Openness does not mean every component must be free of charge, nor does it eliminate intellectual property rights or commercial interests. Companies may create paid services and proprietary products while respecting user rights and declared compatibility rules.

We must clearly distinguish what is enforced by a license, what can be verified technically, and what requires organizational or legal mechanisms.

If an ecosystem participant violates its principles, there must be a transparent review process, an opportunity to respond, a path to remedy the violation, and an appeal mechanism. Serious or repeated violations may result in suspension or revocation of official status. This must not automatically prohibit independent use of open-source code within the terms of its license.

The vision is shared. Implementations can be independent. The choice belongs to the individual.

9. Governance and Accountability

ME should develop as an open international movement supported by an organization that protects its core principles and standards, alongside independent teams building products and services.

The author's role and public representation of the vision must not become unlimited authority over the entire ecosystem.

Governance should provide:

- Public rules and a documented history of significant changes.
- A clear distribution of responsibilities.
- Transparent conflict resolution.
- Independent review of disputed decisions and the possibility of appeal.
- Protection against capture by a single company, group, or leader.
- Succession mechanisms if the founder steps away or changes roles.
- Accountability for product quality, security, and claims about capabilities.

Fundamental human rights and principles of autonomy should receive stronger protection than technical choices and business models, which may change.

Implementation can evolve as technology advances. Changing the foundational principles requires a special process, public discussion, and broad community agreement.

Intelligence, influence, or technical capability alone does not grant the right to govern other people.

10. Accessibility and Economics

The basic ability to maintain personal digital memory and a user-controlled digital identity should be accessible to everyone, regardless of income, country, or social status.

ME should aim to support different devices, limited internet connectivity, local operation where possible, multiple languages, and the needs of people with disabilities.

Language and device support may vary in quality. These limitations must be acknowledged, measured, and gradually reduced.

The ecosystem may be sustained through a combination of:

- Free core features.
- Paid advanced capabilities.
- Commercial products and services.
- Enterprise solutions.
- Grants, donations, and partnerships.
- Voluntary community funds and collaborative initiatives.

A paid subscription must not be a prerequisite for preserving, viewing, exporting, or transferring one's own memory. At the same time, the terms for storing large amounts of data on third-party servers, retention periods, and inactive account policies must be transparent.

People should be able to export their data and store it independently, even after they stop using a service.

The economic model must not depend on selling personal memory, covertly exploiting vulnerabilities, or manipulating users into staying.

Commercial recommendations, sponsorship interests, and personalization limitations should be clear. Users must be able to opt out of personalization based on selected data.

Open source, intellectual property, commercial profit, and public benefit can coexist. Licensing, attribution, and the distribution of benefits must be transparent. Results created for the public good may be supported through open licenses, funds, and other mechanisms for shared access.

11. Law, Human Rights, and Safety

ME must comply with applicable law while being guided by respect for human dignity, human rights, and personal autonomy.

Laws differ between countries. The existence of a government request does not automatically mean that it is consistent with ME's ethical principles.

When handling government and other requests, the system should seek to minimize disclosure of personal data, examine the legal basis through available mechanisms, and challenge unjustified requests where lawful and possible.

ME must not assist in committing crimes, harming other people, or exploiting their vulnerabilities.

Safety rules should distinguish discussion of difficult topics from actionable assistance in causing harm. Restrictions should be proportionate, understandable, and, where possible, accompanied by an explanation, a safe alternative, and a way to appeal.

ME should respect cultural and linguistic diversity, religious and philosophical beliefs, and each person's right to change their worldview. It must not covertly steer users toward a particular ideology.

At the same time, cultural differences do not justify violence or violations of fundamental human rights.

Special Responsibilities

The system should account for the age of its users, protect children and teenagers from exploitation and manipulation, support their growing autonomy, and respect applicable requirements regarding the involvement of parents or legal guardians.

ME should also pursue environmental responsibility by using computing and storage resources efficiently, extending device lifespans, and accounting for the environmental impact of technology.

12. ETERNITY and the Limits of Digital Continuation

Digital legacy requires special responsibility.

ME can help people decide during their lifetime which stories, knowledge, creative works, messages, and other materials they wish to leave behind. It may support instructions concerning access, preservation, deletion, and the creation of an interactive digital representation.

It is essential to distinguish between:

- Preserved documents and memories.
- Reconstructions of a person's communication style and views based on available materials.
- System-generated assumptions about how the person might respond.
- Unknown and unproven questions concerning consciousness and personal identity.

An interactive digital representation must not be presented as the deceased person themselves or as proven continuation of their consciousness.

The system must respect prior instructions, the rights and feelings of loved ones, and the privacy of third parties. Where no instructions exist, further decisions should account for applicable law and the legitimate interests of loved ones without automatically granting them unrestricted access to all data.

ETERNITY should help preserve a legacy without exploiting grief or making false promises of immortality.

13. The First Technological Hypothesis

The broader vision of ME will have little practical value unless it can demonstrate its usefulness through a small, working example.

The first step is therefore not to build all four dimensions at once, but to test whether personal memory improves the quality of AI interactions.

Our first hypothesis:

If AI preserves user-controlled memory, connects information over time, and accounts for changes in context, it can become a more useful personal assistant than a system without such memory.

The first prototype should test three connected capabilities:

1. Personal memory. Preserve selected information from different interactions and use it in later tasks.
2. Continuity. Connect events, goals, and observations over time, recognize changes, and distinguish past information from current circumstances.
3. Contextual usefulness. Use memory to better support learning, creation, exploration, and informed decision-making.

Initially, the user will be the project's founder. The next step will be to involve a small group of people with different tasks and needs.

The prototype must allow users to view, correct, export, and delete memory. It must distinguish facts from assumptions, respect retention boundaries, and give people control over how their information is used.

Success should be measured not only by technical functionality, but also by whether assistance becomes more useful, memories remain accurate, data is easy to control, and risks to privacy and autonomy are kept in check.

Only after testing this hypothesis should we decide which capabilities to develop next.

14. The Roadmap

ME should evolve gradually while preserving the coherence of its long-term vision.

Stage 1. Validate the Foundation

Build and test a working personal-memory prototype. Use it in practice, identify errors, document limitations, and establish initial evidence of usefulness.

Stage 2. Engage Early Participants

Invite a small group of users with different needs. Test usefulness, the clarity of memory controls, trust, privacy, and willingness to use the system.

Stage 3. Build an Open Community

Publish results, discuss principles, and engage developers, researchers, designers, entrepreneurs, and other participants. Develop open standards and compatible implementations.

Stage 4. Establish a Sustainable Product

Test funding models, develop services and partnerships, and avoid turning personal data into the price of access to a basic digital identity.

Stage 5. Expand the Ecosystem

Explore new AI models, offline operation, device integration, physical-world interaction, spatial interfaces, and digital legacy as benefits become demonstrable and safe implementation methods emerge.

Over the first six months, success should mean more than audience growth and visibility. It should include a working prototype, initial validation results, a community of engaged participants, and a concrete plan for the next stage.

15. How We Measure Progress

Audience size, feature count, revenue, and computing power matter to a project, but they do not prove that it serves people.

We should measure:

- How many new opportunities people gain.
- Whether they retain control over memory and digital identity.
- How easily they can change providers or leave the system.
- Whether ME helps them develop, learn, and create.
- Whether it improves decision quality without taking the right to decide away from them.
- Whether it protects privacy and resists manipulation.
- Whether it is accessible to people with different resources and needs.
- Whether it supports voluntary collaboration and public benefit.

We must also measure negative outcomes: dependency on the system, data breaches, memory errors, loss of autonomy, digital inequality, and excessive resource consumption.

ME must acknowledge its mistakes and disclose serious incidents responsibly, notify affected people where necessary, address consequences, and share lessons while protecting privacy and security.

If a direction stops providing value or creates unacceptable risks, it must be changed, paused, or discontinued.

Preserving the project is not more important than fulfilling its purpose. If other approaches eventually serve people better, they should be free to develop — including independently of ME.

16. What Remains Open

This vision establishes a direction but does not claim to have all the answers.

We still need to explore:

- How to technically enable portable digital identity.
- How to manage encryption keys, backups, and recovery reliably.
- How to measure the benefits of personal memory and limit errors.
- How to organize independent security reviews.
- How to distribute authority and resolve disputes in an international community.
- How to sustain funding while keeping core capabilities accessible.
- How to develop compatibility without violating the rights of creators and users.
- How to assess the risks of new forms of digital interaction and human enhancement.
- How to study possible AI consciousness without presenting speculation as proven fact.
- How to evaluate the social and environmental consequences of technological development.

Answers should emerge through research, practical experiments, public discussion, and people's lived experience — not solely from the founder's beliefs or the capabilities of a single AI model.

Human rights, autonomy, privacy, and the ability to leave the system form the foundation. Technical choices, organizational structures, and business models may evolve.

The history of these changes should remain public and understandable.

17. An Invitation to Build the Future

ME begins with a personal question: what changes when AI can do more than answer questions — when it can preserve context chosen by a person, help them grow, and remain under their control?

But this question reaches far beyond a single application.

It concerns who owns digital identity, how technology should affect human freedom, what new forms of interaction between people and AI might emerge, and how to preserve everyone's right to shape their own future.

We do not claim to have all the answers. We propose an open direction that can be explored, challenged, tested, and developed together.

ME should remain compatible with other approaches, open to independent participants, and willing to change when better solutions emerge.

The future should not belong to a single company. It should expand human possibilities.

First, Soul. Then, Body. Then, World. And one day, Eternity.

Let's build that future together.
